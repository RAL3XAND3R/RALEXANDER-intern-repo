# Inclusive Design Reflection

## Vulnerable Populations in Digital Spaces

When we talk about vulnerable populations, we are talking about people who might have a harder time using a product or dealing with certain situations than the average user. This can include people with disabilities, cognitive differences, or other personal circumstances.

For Focus Bear, ADHD, Autism, and executive functioning difficulties are especially relevant because the app is meant to help people with things like focus, productivity, and getting started with tasks.

One thing I learned from this is that you cannot assume that everyone with ADHD or Autism needs the same thing. Two people can have the same diagnosis and still have completely different experiences. Something that helps one person could annoy or overwhelm someone else.

Because of that, I think the goal should be to give people a clear and flexible experience instead of trying to find one solution that supposedly works for everyone.

## Designing for Neurodivergent Users

I think one of the easiest mistakes when designing an app is adding too much stuff.

More buttons, more notifications, more settings, more features... sometimes that can actually make things worse. If someone already has trouble focusing or deciding what to do next, giving them ten different options might not be very helpful.

For me, a good experience would be one where the user does not have to stop and think too much about how the app works. Things should be clear enough that they can understand what is happening and what they can do next.

The way the app communicates also matters. If someone is already struggling with a task, the last thing they probably need is an app making them feel guilty about it. The goal should be to help them move forward, even if the step they take is small.

## Supporting Neurodivergent Team Members

The same thing applies when working with other people.

Not everyone likes to communicate or work in the same way. Some people might prefer written instructions because they can go back and read them later. Others might prefer talking things through. Some people may answer immediately, while others might need some time to think before responding.

I do not think there is one "correct" way to work. The important thing is being clear and being willing to adapt when necessary.

Sometimes a small change in how something is explained can make it much easier for another person to understand what is expected from them.

## How I Can Make My Communication More Inclusive

One thing I can work on is being clearer.

Sometimes I might understand what I mean in my own head but forget that the other person does not have the same context. So instead of assuming they already know what I am talking about, I can explain the situation a little better.

This is especially important when discussing technical problems. Saying "this isn't working" does not help much. It is better to explain what I was trying to do, what happened, and what I have already tried.

I can also remember that people do not always process information at the same speed or in the same way. Being patient and giving someone space to understand something is sometimes better than expecting an immediate answer.

## Things That Could Make Focus Bear Less Helpful

There are a few things that I think could easily make a productivity app more stressful instead of helpful:

Too many notifications.
Too many things happening on the screen.
Instructions that are confusing or vague.
Changing how something works without making it clear.
Asking the user to remember too many things.
Making the user feel bad for not completing a task.

The last one stands out to me because someone using Focus Bear might already be frustrated with themselves for not getting something done. If the app adds more pressure or guilt, it could have the opposite effect of what it is supposed to do.

## One Practical Change I Can Make

As a Backend Developer Intern, I am obviously not going to be designing every screen in the app, but the backend can still affect how frustrating or smooth the experience feels.

One thing I can pay attention to is what happens when something goes wrong.

For example, if an action fails, the system should make it possible for the app to tell the user what happened instead of just giving them a random error or leaving them wondering if anything worked.

Something as simple as a clear error message can make a difference. If I am already having trouble focusing and an app suddenly does something unexpected, I do not want to sit there trying to figure out what happened.

## What I Learned From Other People's Experiences

After reading about people with ADHD and Autism using productivity tools, something that stood out to me was that productivity tools themselves can sometimes become another problem.

A tool can have calendars, reminders, categories, statistics, goals, timers, streaks, and a hundred settings. At some point, the person might end up spending more time managing the productivity tool than actually doing what they wanted to do.

That made sense to me. More features do not automatically mean something is better.

Sometimes just showing the user one clear next step might be more useful than giving them a whole system to manage.

## One Possible Improvement for Focus Bear

One thing that could be useful is giving users more control over notifications and reminders.

Some people probably need reminders because otherwise they might forget something. But for someone else, getting notifications all the time might become annoying or distracting.

Instead of deciding that everyone needs the same number of reminders, giving the user more control could make the app work better for different people.

## A Supportive Response to a User

Imagine that a user says:

> "I have been avoiding my tasks all day, and now I feel overwhelmed because I have too much to do. I don't even know where to start."

I would respond with something like:

> "Yeah, that sounds really overwhelming. You don't have to figure everything out right now. Maybe just pick one small thing to start with, even if it's only the first step of a bigger task. Once you get that done, you can decide what to do next."

I would rather respond like that than tell someone to "be more productive" or "just start working." If starting were that easy, they probably would not be asking for help in the first place.

## Final Reflection

The main thing I got from this topic is that accessibility is not always about adding some special feature.

Sometimes it is just about not making things harder than they need to be.

Clear instructions, predictable behaviour, fewer unnecessary distractions, and giving people some flexibility can help neurodivergent users, but honestly, those things can make an app better for almost everyone.

As a developer, I usually think first about whether something works. But something can technically work perfectly and still be confusing or frustrating for the person using it.

That is something I want to keep in mind while working at Focus Bear. Even if I am mostly working on the backend, there is still a real person on the other side of whatever I build.
